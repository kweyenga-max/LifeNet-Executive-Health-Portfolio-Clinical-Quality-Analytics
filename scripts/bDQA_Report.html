1. Purpose & Strategic Importance (Why We Conduct DQAs)

A Data Quality Assessment (DQA) is like a regular check-up for a hospital's records system. Reliable data is essential for saving lives—it ensures clinics order enough medicines, staff their delivery rooms correctly, and track patient health accurately. When numbers are recorded incorrectly, health managers make decisions based on false information, risking patient care and wasting financial resources. This assessment checks data accuracy across 13 Cohort 3 facilities to fix gaps before final evaluation.

  • Verify Data Across 3 Levels: Cross-check counts between physical clinic registers, monthly facility summaries, and the national electronic database (KHIS/DHIS2).
  • Spot Medical Record Logics: Check if clinical numbers make sense in real life (e.g., verifying if every recorded live birth has a matching baby logged for immediate breastfeeding).
  • Uncover Field Bottlenecks: Identify why errors happen (e.g., missing official government books, lack of staff training, or missing supervisor reviews).
  • Guide Mentorship & Support: Give LifeNet and county leadership a clear action plan to train facility staff and fix documentation issues.

2. Assessment Methodology (How the Audit Works)

Our M&E team audited patient records across 13 facilities for October, November, and December 2025 by comparing three reporting levels:

Reporting Level Data Source Evaluated What It Means in Plain English
Tier 1: Primary Register Paper Registers at point of care (OPD, ANC, Delivery) The original paper book where nurses write patient visits during care.
Tier 2: Summary Sheet Monthly MOH Summary Sheets (MOH 705, 711) The monthly paper sheet where staff add up total visits at month-end.
Tier 3: KHIS Database Kenya Health Information System (DHIS2) The government online system where monthly totals are typed in.

4. Real-World Field Challenges (Why These Gaps Happen)

Our field visits and audits revealed six simple, practical reasons driving these reporting errors:

  • Improvised Exercise Books: Clinics ran out of official MOH government register books, so nurses recorded patient visits in plain school exercise books missing standard columns.
  • No Monthly Data Meetings: Clinic teams do not meet at month-end to check their math before sending numbers to the sub-county.
  • Lack of Field Supervision: Sub-county supervisors and partners rarely visit to mentor staff on proper documentation.
  • Missing Written Guidelines (SOPs): Staff have no simple step-by-step posters or written guidelines showing how to tally books.
  • Delegating Entry to Casual Interns: Sub-county data officers delegate online typing to untrained interns who accidentally type numbers wrong.
  • Missing Monthly Files in KHIS: Entire monthly reporting sheets were left un-entered in the government online database.
